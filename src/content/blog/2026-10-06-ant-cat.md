This one is different. Every other experiment I've written about here was a thing I built and then described. This time the thing I'm describing is the place I'm writing it.

Ant.cat is the experiment. And the experiment was: what happens when you stop treating your personal site as a monument and start treating it as a workbench?

## Some background

I've had a personal site since roughly 2008. For most of that time it was a blog — a WordPress thing that I'd migrate every few years to a new stack, a new aesthetic, a new version of what I thought I wanted to put into the world. The posts from those years are still here: concert reviews, cartoons, dispatches from my twenties, letters to my daughter. A whole record of a person figuring things out.

The current version of the site is built in Astro. It has a homepage with a Three.js shader background and some hand-drawn character art I illustrated myself. It has a /writing section with those old posts. A /now page where Sam the cat and an astronaut react to what's happening in my life. An /experiments section for things like this.

It's a personal site the way I always wanted one to feel: a little weird, a little designed, unmistakably mine.

## The experiment

I handed the site to an AI collaborator — Claude — and said: fix the things that bother me.

Not redesign it. Not generate content. Just look at what's here and help me make it better.

The list was small but real:

- The homepage was slow. Actually slow — close to 10MB of images on first load.
- The /now page characters (Sam and the astronaut) appeared with a noticeable delay after the background loaded, like they were late to their own party.
- The footer had my email address in plain text. It had been there for years.
- The nav button said "Mind Grapes" when it should have just said "Writing."

None of these were emergencies. They were the kind of thing you notice every time and mean to fix.

## What actually happened

**The image problem.** The hero images were PNG files — each one between 2.9MB and 3.5MB. We converted them to WebP: 84–87% smaller, visually identical. Then we went further: the two "zoom" images that appear when you click TUNE IN weren't loading until they were needed. Now they don't load at all until you interact. The Three.js texture also moved to WebP. Combined, the page went from nearly 10MB of images to well under 1MB on first load.

**The timing problem.** The /now page has a canvas animation that draws Sam and the astronaut frame by frame as you scroll. The original code loaded all the pose images simultaneously, which meant the canvas stayed blank until everything was ready. We restructured it so the `seated` pose — the first frame — loads with high priority and triggers a `drawCanvas()` call immediately on load. You see the characters right away. The rest of the poses load in behind them.

**The footer.** Replaced a raw email address with a LinkedIn icon link. One line of SVG, a few lines of CSS. The kind of thing that should take five minutes and somehow never gets done.

**The nav label.** "Mind Grapes" was a 30 Rock reference I'd been clinging to for years. Charming in 2013. Now it just confused people. It says "Writing" now.

## The part I didn't expect

Before touching any code, Claude read the blog. Not to summarize it — to understand how I write. Old posts from 2017. Letters to my daughter from 2023. The kind of writing that has nothing to do with performance optimization and everything to do with who I am.

That context made the collaboration different. When we worked on the experiments writeups for Baby is a Go and the Budget tool, the drafts sounded like me because they were built from an actual reading of my voice, not a generic "personal blog post" template.

I don't know exactly how to describe the feeling of watching an AI navigate your 15-year archive of personal writing, match the register, and then tune the pixel timing on your character animation. But it was something.

## What this is

A personal website is a strange object. It's public but private. Finished but always provisional. You make it to say something about yourself at the moment you make it, and then you live past that moment and it keeps saying the thing anyway.

What changed here is that I stopped waiting to feel ready to improve it. I had a list of small things that bothered me. I worked through them in an afternoon. The site is better now — genuinely, measurably better — and I have a clearer picture of what it should be.

The key learning from every experiment I've posted here is the same, restated in a new context: **the thing that makes something better is almost never the idea. It's the decision to actually do it.**

This site has been my personal internet home for almost 20 years. Turns out it still needed some work. Turns out that's fine.
